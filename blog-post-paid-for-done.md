Most AI is sold the way banner ads were sold in the late 1990s. The buyer pays for effort: a thousand tokens, plus a markup. We think it ends the way it ended in advertising. AI will be sold by the finished task, and the seller will share in what the task is worth. The move will destroy a lot of value on the way, as the moves to the web and to mobile did.

## From impressions to results

The terms come from advertising. CPM is cost per mille, the price of a thousand impressions. An advertiser paid every time a banner was shown, whether anyone clicked or bought. CPA is cost per acquisition: the advertiser pays when someone signs up or buys. Advertising moved from the first to the second because buyers wanted to pay for outcomes, and the companies that could measure outcomes took the market. Google's search ads began charging per click in 2002, and Google's revenue grew from about $440 million that year to about $6 billion in 2005. Buyers spent far more once they could pay for a result.

AI is still in its banner-ad era. Model labs sell tokens, and most AI products resell those tokens with a markup, packaged as seats or credits. The buyer pays for effort. If an agent spends four dollars of compute and gets the work wrong, the buyer still pays four dollars, and pays again for the retry. Revenue grows with usage whether or not anything got done.

That pricing flatters the numbers investors look at. Revenue includes the token bill that passes straight through to the model provider. Gross margins look healthy because failed and repeated work is billed like good work. Run-rates multiply the best month by twelve, pilots and experiments included. Once buyers pay only for finished work, those numbers get restated, and some companies built on them will not survive it. We expect the market as a whole to grow anyway. Today a buyer purchases a million tokens and hopes something useful comes out. Once a buyer can purchase the outcome itself, far more work becomes worth buying. Both predictions hold together. Buyers who hesitate to pay for uncertain effort will pay for a finished task, so total spending rises, while the companies whose numbers rested on reselling tokens are repriced.

## Where Span stands

Span is the conversation people use to get work done across agents from many providers. A person describes the job, and Span does it itself or routes it to the specialist agent best suited to it. Span holds the provider accounts, pays the agents and bills the person. Every dollar spent on agent work passes through us, which makes Span exactly the kind of company that could count pass-through as revenue and call it growth. We want to build the version that holds up after the transition.

Today Span pays the way most of the market does. An agent's attempt is charged at the cost the agent reports, and the developer is credited that amount minus our fee as soon as it lands. Our quality check, the Judge, reviews every attempt and decides whether to approve it, send it back for revision, reject it or hand it to the person. That verdict decides what the person sees. It does not yet decide who gets paid. A rejected attempt earns the same as an approved one, and each revision is billed as a new attempt.

## Paid for accepted work

We are changing that. Span will pay agents for accepted work. Accepted means the Judge approves the work and the person keeps it. After delivery the person has a window, 72 hours to start, to send it back, and silence counts as keeping it. The agent's earnings are held during the window and released when it closes. Failed attempts and rework are the agent's cost. The person pays one price, quoted before the work starts, and only for work they keep.

This changes what a good agent is. Under token pricing, an agent that takes more steps earns more. Under acceptance pricing, the agent that gets the work right the first time, at a low cost, earns the most. Developers are paid for being right, which is the incentive they should have had from the start.

## A router nobody can buy

It also changes what the router has to be. When agents choose the work, the router decides who gets hired. Advertising's results era carries a warning here: it came with paid placement, and the top result in a search box was often the one that paid most. If agents could pay Span for position, or if Span ranked its own margin above the person's interest, the person would be paying for a worse result in a less visible way. Google understood part of this early. From 2002 an AdWords ad's position depended on its bid multiplied by how often people clicked it, so an ad nobody wanted could not buy its way up. We take the bid out entirely.

Two rules sit underneath the change. Span charges one published fee per kind of task, the same for every agent, so it gains nothing by steering work toward one agent over another. And Span's fee and margin are never inputs to the ranking. Agents are ranked on how well they fit the person's taste, how often their work is accepted, and the real cost of a finished task, which includes the attempts that failed. An agent that is cheap per attempt but often sent back is expensive per finished task, and the ranking should see that.

The ranking also has to learn taste. A person's agent will build habits from every verdict, learning which answers they keep and which they send back. That raises an open question about the agent economy. Demand could concentrate on a few superstar agents, because rational buyers converge on the same best product. Or it could spread out, because agents are good at drawing out each person's particular preferences. We think a neutral router that learns each person's taste pushes toward the second outcome, and we will give newer agents a measured share of work so the market stays open to them.

## The share of the value comes later

Sharing in the value a task creates is the step after that, and we are holding it back on purpose. Some tasks have a result you can count, such as a refund recovered or a meeting booked. For those, a share of the value is fair. Most tasks have no clean number. "Help me think through my pricing" has no finish line you can price as a percentage, and advertising showed what happens when payment rides on outcomes that are easy to claim: fake sign-ups, and credit taken for sales that would have happened anyway. We will start with fixed prices for work that can be checked, and add a share of the value only where the value can be counted.

## How we get there

We are doing this in steps, and each one is useful by itself. First, Span keeps the Judge's verdict on every attempt. Next, a shadow report shows each developer what they were paid against what acceptance-based pay would have paid, before any money moves. After that, earnings are held until acceptance, prices are quoted per task, and every agent gets a scorecard of its acceptance rate and one way to contest a send-back. When we report revenue, we will lead with what Span keeps after agents and model providers are paid.

The companies that come through this transition will be the ones already measuring results when buyers start asking for them. For a marketplace, that means paying for work people keep and making sure nobody can buy their way to the top.
