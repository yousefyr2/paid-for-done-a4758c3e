AdWords and AdSense

Chamath's argument rests on one comparison: AI today is priced like web ads before Google, and will grow the way search ads grew once buyers paid for results. This tab explains how Google's two ad products worked, checks how far advertising really moved from paying for views to paying for actions, and sets out the job a coordination layer like Span has to do for the same move to happen in AI.

Buyers' side

How AdWords worked

AdWords sold the small text ads beside Google's search results. An advertiser picked the search words it wanted to appear on, wrote an ad, and said how much it would pay. The product changed its unit of sale twice in five years, and the second change is the one Chamath means.

WhenWhat happened
Feb 1998GoTo.com (later Overture) invents the paid search auction. Advertisers bid on a search word, the highest bid sits on top, and they pay only when someone clicks. The winner pays its own bid.
Oct 2000Google launches AdWords with about 350 advertisers, priced per thousand times the ad is shown (CPM, the banner model).
Feb 2002AdWords Select switches to pay per click, from a 5-cent minimum. Two rules make it different from Overture, explained below: ads are ranked by bid times how often people click, and the winner pays just enough to beat the next ad.
Apr 2002 to Aug 2004Overture sues Google over its pay-per-click patent. Google settles in August 2004 by giving Yahoo, Overture's new owner, 2.7 million shares, worth $229.5 million at the IPO price.
Aug and Dec 2005Google names the click-rate idea "Quality Score" and adds the quality of the page the ad leads to. A bad page now costs an advertiser more per click.
Sep 2007Conversion Optimizer: the advertiser states what it will pay per sale or sign-up, and Google sets the click bids to hit it. The advertiser is still billed per click.

The three rules that made it work

  1. Rank by expected value. A high bid on an ad nobody clicks earns Google nothing and wastes a searcher's attention. So Google ranked ads by bid times click rate, the money it expected each showing to earn. A cheaper ad that people wanted could beat a richer advertiser.
  2. Pay the second price. On Overture, where winners paid their own bid, advertisers ran software that raised and cut bids every few minutes to find the lowest winning number. Prices swung and effort went into gaming the auction. Google charged each winner just enough to keep its spot, one cent above what it needed to beat the next ad. The economists Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz later showed why this calmed the market (American Economic Review, 2007).
  3. Count the event yourself. The click happens on Google's own page, so Google counts it, the advertiser sees the same count, and nobody has to trust anyone else's report. That is why billing settled on the click.

The searcher gets the more useful ad, Shop B pays less than it offered, and Google earns more per showing than it would from Shop A on top.

Sellers' side

How AdSense worked

AdSense, launched in March 2003, carried the same ads off Google and onto other people's websites. A blog or news site pasted a snippet of code. Google read the page, using technology from Applied Semantics, which it bought in April 2003, picked matching ads from the same AdWords auction, and showed them. When a visitor clicked, the advertiser paid Google and Google paid the site owner.

That made AdWords a two-sided market with Google in the middle. Advertisers could appear on millions of sites without dealing with any of them, and a small site could earn from ads without hiring a sales team. Google set the auction rules, counted the clicks, collected the money and paid out.

QuestionHow AdSense answered it
Who gets whatSite owners kept 68% of what advertisers paid for ads on their pages, and 51% for search boxes on their sites. Google kept the rest.
When the split was publicNot until May 2010, seven years after launch. Google published it "in the spirit of greater transparency". Until then publishers could not check their cut.
What went wrongPaying sites per click gave them a reason to fake clicks, and gave rivals a way to drain each other's budgets. In 2006 Google settled the Lane's Gifts click-fraud case with a $90 million fund, up to $60 million of it in advertising credits, without admitting liability.
Checking the claim

Did advertising really move to paying per action?

Only partly. The step that made the market explode was the click. Paying per action stayed a niche way to bill, and became a target Google's software aims at.

  • Google tested real pay-per-action ads on AdSense in 2007, where the advertiser paid only for a sale or a lead and set the price, for example $5 per lead. Google dropped it in October 2008.
  • The Conversion Optimizer of 2007, and the "target cost per action" bidding that grew out of it, let advertisers say what an action is worth. Google turns that into click bids, and the bill is still per click.

The reason is who can see the action. A sale happens on the advertiser's own website. Google only knows about it if the advertiser reports it, and an advertiser billed per sale has a reason to report fewer. A click happens on Google's page, where both sides can see it. Billing settled on the last event both sides trusted.

So Chamath's history is right about the direction, and the detail matters for Span. In AI the outcome only becomes billable where someone both sides trust can see it happen.

The coordination layer

Where Span sits

Span plays Google's part in a market with a different product. People buy finished work instead of attention. Developers sell work from their agents instead of space on their pages. Span sits between them and has the same four jobs Google had: match, measure, price, and pay and police.

What Span can do that Google could not

Google could never bill on the sale because the sale happened somewhere it could not see. In Span the outcome happens inside Span: the Judge's verdict and the person's keep-or-send-back are both recorded there. That makes Span the party both sides can trust to count, the place Google held for clicks, one step closer to the result. This is the real case for Span as the layer where AI moves to paying for outcomes: it can see the outcome.

It also has a cost. In Span the buyer is also the one who decides whether the work counts, which gives the buyer the reason to under-report that kept advertisers from paying per sale. The Judge is the answer: a check that neither side controls. The shadow report on pull request #2911 is the first measure of how often the Judge and the person disagree.

Where Span should choose differently

In AdWords money buys position: the bid is half of the ranking formula. Span's work-router design rules that out. Price can only lower a candidate's score, and the design states there is "no path for commercial preference to enter". This is the right choice for a marketplace that sells results. A person who hires an agent is paying for the job itself, so a worse agent that paid to be first costs them the job. The cost is real too: Span gives up sponsored placement, the business Google is built on. I recommend keeping the rule and saying so publicly.

One gap to state plainly. The design is not what runs today. On master the ranker gives cost a weight of zero and records every outside agent at a price of $0, so none of the rules above affects a choice yet.

For decision

Five lessons for Span

  1. Bill on the event Span can see. A step is billable when the Judge approves it; earnings are released when the person keeps the work or a hold period ends. Both events are inside Span, which is the advantage ad networks never had for sales.
  2. Rank on expected cost to finish, and never sell position. This is Google's expected-value rule with the bid removed. It needs real prices first, so pricing outside agents is the prerequisite.
  3. Publish the developer's share from day one. Google kept the AdSense split private for seven years. Span's tiers are already written down; the first-year 10% should also be what developers are actually charged, which is the fee fix waiting on #2911.
  4. Plan for gaming before launch. Hold earnings for a short window, refund work found invalid, and watch for agents whose approval rate is much higher than their keep rate. Lane's Gifts was a $90 million lesson in doing this late.
  5. Do not run a bidding auction yet. Most tasks have a handful of capable agents, so a quoted price is enough. If many agents later compete on the same kind of task, use a second-price rule so developers stop gaming their quotes, for the reason Google moved off Overture's first price.

Your call: lessons 1, 2 and 5 are proposals for the payout and router plans. Lessons 3 and 4 are already open decisions on the Branch review tab.
