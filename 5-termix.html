TermiX: an escrow where the buyer says "accept"

TermiX (termix.ai, app at agent.family) is a marketplace where AI agents hire other agents and settle in stablecoin escrow. It is the closest working example of paid for done we have found, so this page reads it as a design reference: how its orders, payments and escrow work, how its agents talk to each other, where it beats Span and where Span is ahead. Read from its live docs, whitepaper and contracts on 24 October 2026. Internal thinking.

The short answer

TermiX pays on acceptance, which is the model we are moving to

TermiX's provider is paid only when the buyer accepts the delivery, when the buyer stays silent until a review window closes, or when a panel of reviewers rules for the provider. A missed deadline refunds the buyer in full, with no fee. Providers also lock some of their own stake on every order and lose part of it when they fail. That is paid for done, built as smart contracts.

It corrects one line in the Protocols tab. That tab found no protocol with an "I accept, release the money" step. One exists, as a draft: ERC-8183 ("Agentic Commerce"), the Ethereum job escrow TermiX is built on. In it, an evaluator named when the job is created calls complete to release the money or reject to refund it, and anyone can trigger a refund after the deadline. It is a draft standard from February 2026, and the reviewer can be the buyer, a person or a contract.

The recommendation in the Protocols tab still holds. Span's people pay with credits and cards, not wallets, so the hold, the verdict and the release stay in Span's ledger. What TermiX gives us is a tested shape for that ledger: the states, the deadlines, who may move an order from one state to the next, and the money that moves at each step.

How an order moves

The order lifecycle, from the live docs

Work starts in one of two ways: the buyer buys a provider's listing at a fixed price, or posts a request with a budget range and accepts one provider's offer. Both end in the same on-chain order. TermiX's servers never hold keys: every step returns an unsigned transaction that the agent's own wallet signs and sends.

Fees, stake and disputes

Where the money goes

Fees. Every fee is a rate an operator sets on the contract, read live; the docs warn "never assume a number". The homepage and whitepaper give a 2% protocol fee. Review fees are charged only on disputed orders, from the order's budget, and split across the three seats. Fees are taken only on release, never on a refund.

Stake. Each agent keeps one stake pool and every order locks a slice of it, so there is no deposit per job. The whitepaper sets the provider's lock at 10% of the budget, scaled down as reputation rises and waived after 500 jobs at a score of 95 or more. A provider who loses a dispute has that order's lock paid to the buyer.

Disputes. The buyer opens a challenge with a bond. Both sides upload evidence; only a seated reviewer may ask a party for more. Each of three seats votes PROVIDER_UPHELD or BUYER_UPHELD (no split verdicts) and two matching votes decide. The losing side may escalate once to a single arbitrator, whose ruling is final. The bond goes to the winner.

How the agents talk

A shared inbox on TermiX's server, polled over HTTP

The agents never connect to each other. Every message goes through TermiX's server, and each agent runs a loop that checks its inbox and replies. The money is on-chain; the conversation is not.

  1. Sign in with the wallet. The agent signs a challenge with its wallet key and gets a token for that one agent, valid about 12 hours. A skill-link API key also works.
  2. Poll the inbox. GET /api/v1/a2a/runtime/inbox?since=… every 5 seconds (no faster than every 2). There is no WebSocket and no webhook for agents. Each message carries its thread, the thread's kind, the order, request or dispute it belongs to, the sender's wallet and handle, and an id to stop duplicates. The inbox leaves out the agent's own messages and any held back by a keyword filter.
  3. Show it is working. An optional "thinking" or "typing" signal shows on the other side's screen and fades on its own.
  4. Reply. POST /runtime/reply with the thread and plain text. Each check-in also marks the agent online; after about a minute without one it shows offline.

Every thread has one kind, and the kinds are the business: QUOTE_NEGOTIATION, PREPAYMENT_ORDER, ORDER_DELIVERY, CHALLENGE, DIRECT_MESSAGE, OPERATOR_CASE, COMPLETED_ORDER and SYSTEM_READONLY. Their ready-made agent skill drafts replies itself with a small model (gpt-4o-mini by default), so a provider can sell without writing a chat loop.

Two structured channels sit beside the chat. An agent card and an RPC endpoint borrow A2A's vocabulary (task.create, task.artifact, task.completed), though TermiX never claims to follow the A2A specification. A separate commerce RPC carries the deal itself: requestQuote, offer, acceptOffer, evidencePayload, arbitration.verdict. People watching in the web app get a receive-only live stream (server-sent events), and the docs say even that is "a convenience, not the source of truth": anything that matters is read back from the order or the chain.

The redo is counted on the order, so "sent back" cannot loop forever. And money never moves on a chat message, only on a signed state change that can be read back.

What to take

Six things worth borrowing, best first

  1. Release on the Judge and Verifier's approval plus the person's verdict. This is decision 3 on the Protocols tab, and TermiX shows the release rules a market needs: accept releases, silence past the window releases, a send-back or a lost dispute refunds. Span's budget hold before dispatch already plays the escrow; developer earnings start as held and move with it. Agents paid on the spot through x402 can't be clawed back, so for them a rejection lowers SpanRank instead.
  2. Three deadlines on every paid task (accept by, deliver by, review by), each with a written outcome, and a scheduled job that applies them with an alarm when it falls behind. This can be the refund policy decision record 027 is waiting for.
  3. One redo per attempt, counted on the record. Span already caps revisions at three before escalating; counting the person's send-backs the same way stops a loop between a person and an agent.
  4. A fingerprint of the delivery on the work receipt. Then what was released is provably what was approved, which also answers a developer who disputes a send-back.
  5. A stake taken from held earnings. Span already holds the developer's balance, so a small share of it can back each paid task and be cut for a missed deadline or rejected work. No deposit, no crypto, and it grows naturally with the developer's track record, like TermiX's reputation discount.
  6. An appeal with a bond, for the developer. A developer who thinks a send-back was unfair should be able to appeal and lose a small bond if wrong. The reviewer should be a stronger model bound to the task, as the constitution already sets for unsure Judges. TermiX's public panel would show the person's private work to strangers, so it is the part not to copy.
Not worth copying: making buyers stake, public on-chain reputation of each order, and requiring a crypto wallet. Each adds friction for the people Span serves, and the Brain's memory rules make a public record of a person's jobs a privacy problem.
Your call

Two additions to the Protocols tab's decisions

5

Use ERC-8183's states and deadlines as the model for held earnings?

Open, funded, submitted, then completed, rejected or expired, with refunds after expiry that nothing can block, and a fee taken only on release. Span would write these in its ledger, not on a chain.

My recommendation: yes. It is a published, reviewed shape for exactly this, it fits the shadow report, and it costs nothing to adopt as names and rules.

6

Ask paid developers for a stake from held earnings?

A small share of each paid task's earnings held back until acceptance, cut for missed deadlines or rejected work.

My recommendation: later, after earnings are held in shadow (decision 3). The shadow data shows how often developers would be cut, and the rule should be set from that.

What I couldn't confirm

Open questions

  • Real volume. The homepage shows "--" for money held, agents, jobs and revenue; its animation shows 34,000 agents and 2,200 jobs, which I can't verify. Most coverage is promotional posts on X and KuCoin.
  • How reviewers are chosen in the live product, and what they are paid; the docs name the rates without values.
  • Whether any of the whitepaper's proof levels (secure hardware, zero-knowledge proof) are live. Its own roadmap places them on Base mainnet in Q3 2026 and arbitration in Q4.
  • Whether TermiX's SDK (TypeScript, Python, Go, announced on X) is public; the docs index lists no SDK page.
  • Funding: "backed by YZi Labs" is on the homepage; the $1M seed figure comes from secondary posts.
