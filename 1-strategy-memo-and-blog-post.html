Span · pricing, payouts, routing and taste · strategy memo

Paid for Done

Two shifts are coming at once. AI will be paid for finished work instead of by the token, and AI agents will increasingly decide who gets the work instead of people clicking the first result they see. Span sits where both meet: it finds agents, chooses one, hires it and pays it. This memo explains both shifts in full, records exactly what Span's code does today, and specifies the version I'd build. Everything rests on one rule: Span must work for the person, not for whoever pays it most.

1 · Shift one · how work is paid

From paying for effort to paying for results

"Current State of the Union of AI/SI: Most everyone is selling CPM. Cost per thousand tokens + markup.

Future State of the Union of AI/SI: Everyone will sell CPA. Accomplish the task, participate in the ROI.

In the transition from CPM to CPA, we will debunk much of the current gamesmanship around revenues, gross margins and run-rates. This will necessarily involve a lot of value destruction. See Web1 and Mobile transitions for examples.

Good luck to all the players!"

Chamath Palihapitiya (@chamath), on X, 3 October 2026. View the post. The CPM and CPA definitions in this memo come from this post.

Chamath Palihapitiya is a venture investor (Social Capital), a co-host of the All-In podcast and an early Facebook executive who ran its growth team. The post is about how AI is priced: what model labs, AI apps and agents charge for. It borrows two terms from online advertising as shorthand; it is not about advertising itself. It makes four short claims. Here is what each line means:

The lineWhat it means
"Current State of the Union of AI/SI"Where the AI business stands today. "SI" most likely means superintelligence; he is talking about everyone who sells AI, from model labs to the apps built on them.
"Most everyone is selling CPM. Cost per thousand tokens + markup."Today almost every AI company is paid for usage. Model labs sell tokens by the thousand or million; most AI apps and agents buy those tokens and resell them with a markup, priced per seat, per credit or per call. Revenue rises with how much the AI is used, whether or not the work succeeds.
"Everyone will sell CPA. Accomplish the task…"The price will attach to the finished job instead of the effort: a fixed fee per completed task, owed only if the task is done.
"…participate in the ROI."The step past that: the seller takes a share of what the work was worth to the buyer (money recovered, sales made, hours saved), like a commission or a performance fee.
"…debunk much of the current gamesmanship around revenues…"Revenue is flattered because many companies count the full token bill they pass through to model providers as their own revenue.
"…gross margins…"Margins look healthy because cost simply passes to the buyer with a markup, and failed or repeated work is billed too. Under CPA the seller pays for its own failures, so the margin shows how good the product really is.
"…and run-rates."A run-rate is one month's revenue multiplied by twelve. It is inflated by pilots, experiments and one-off spending that may not repeat. Paying per finished task shows what customers keep paying for.
"This will necessarily involve a lot of value destruction."Company valuations built on those flattering numbers will fall when the numbers are restated on results. Some companies will shrink and some will not survive.
"See Web1 and Mobile transitions for examples."It has happened before. He does not name the examples; the likely ones are the dot-com bust (2000 to 2002), when companies valued on traffic and page views collapsed once the market priced on revenue and profit, and the move to phones, when companies that led on desktop and did not adapt lost their place to those built for mobile.
"Good luck to all the players!"A dry sign-off: nobody now selling AI is safe from the shift, including the biggest names.

Why it matters to Span. A broker is exactly where this "gamesmanship" can hide. If Span reported every dollar that passes through it to agents and model providers as its own revenue, it would be showing CPM-style numbers. The memo's answer is to be paid for accepted work and to report revenue net of what goes to agents and providers (see "How Span reports revenue" in section 5).

The follow-up: AdWords and the size of the market (20 October 2026)

"When Google invented and scaled AdWords and AdSense, the market exploded… because we went from buying 1,000 ads to just getting an outcome. Right now we buy a million tokens. We will eventually move to an outcome. And I think when that happens, this market will just go absolutely crazy."

Chamath Palihapitiya, in a video clip posted by Social Capital (@socialcapital) on X, 20 October 2026. View the clip. The words are as transcribed in that post. The video could not be played for this memo, so check the quote against it before publishing.

Where it is from. The clip is most likely from the All-In episode published 17 October 2026, "CoreWeave Crash, AI Trade Cracks, Anthropic IPO Red Flags, Bill Gates Doomer," with Ben Horowitz as guest. A third-party summary of that episode paraphrases the same prediction: AI pricing will move from cost per million tokens to cost per click and cost per action, the way AdWords and AdSense changed internet advertising, and the market will expand sharply when it does. The match between clip and episode is likely but not confirmed.

Here is what each part of the clip says, and what it adds to the 3 October post:

The lineWhat it means
"When Google invented and scaled AdWords and AdSense, the market exploded…"This time he names his precedent. AdWords (launched 2000) sold ads beside Google search results. AdSense (2003) placed the same ads on other websites and paid those sites a share. Google's revenue went from about $440 million in 2002 to about $6 billion in 2005, almost all of it from these two products.
"…because we went from buying 1,000 ads to just getting an outcome."His reason for the growth: advertisers spent far more once they paid for a result instead of for exposure. The first AdWords ads were sold per thousand views; from 2002 advertisers paid per click. A click is one step short of a sale, which is why the episode summary describes the path as cost per click first and cost per action after.
"Right now we buy a million tokens."The same CPM point as the first post. Model prices are quoted per million tokens today.
"We will eventually move to an outcome."He gives no date. "Eventually" admits the change will take time.
"…this market will just go absolutely crazy."The new claim. The 3 October post warned of value destruction for today's sellers; this one says total spending on AI grows sharply once buyers pay for results. Both can hold at once: buyers who hesitate to pay for uncertain effort will pay for a finished task, so the market grows, while companies valued on token resale are repriced.

What the clip means for Span. Two lessons carry over from AdWords. The first is demand: if paying for results makes buyers spend more, the new spending goes to whoever can price and check results, and Span's accepted-work model is built for that. The second is ranking. From 2002 an AdWords ad's position depended on its bid multiplied by how often people clicked it, so a high bidder with an ad nobody wanted sank. That kept the ads useful enough for the market to grow. Span takes bids out of ranking entirely (see section 3). The click has a Span equivalent too: the Judge's approval is the click, and the person keeping the work is the sale. Span pays on the sale.

He uses them about AI, but the two terms come from online advertising. CPM (cost per mille, meaning per thousand) is paying for every thousand times an ad is shown, whether anyone buys. CPA (cost per acquisition) is paying only when someone actually signs up or buys. Advertising moved from the first to the second because buyers wanted to pay for results, and the companies that could measure results (Google, Facebook) took the market.

In AI, "CPM" is selling tokens: the chunks of text a model reads and writes, priced per thousand or per million with a markup on top. The buyer pays for the agent's effort. If it burns $4 of tokens and fails, the buyer still pays $4. "CPA" means the buyer pays when the task is done. "Participate in the ROI" (return on investment) goes one step further: take a share of the value the task created.

What the two terms mean at Span.

CPM at Span (how it works today) Fact Span pays for effort. Each attempt an agent makes is charged at the agent's reported cost, and the developer is credited that amount minus Span's fee (10%, 15% or 20%) before the quality check has run. An attempt the Judge rejects is charged and credited exactly like one it approves, and every revision is another fully charged attempt. The "markup" in the tweet is Span's fee on the agent's side, plus margins on some tools (Brave Search is listed at cost plus 50%).

CPA at Span (the proposal) Proposal Span pays for accepted work. The "acquisition" is an accepted task: the Judge approves it and the person doesn't send it back within the window (say 72 hours; saying nothing counts as yes). The person pays one price, quoted before the work starts, and only for accepted work. The agent's earnings are held until then and released on acceptance. Failed attempts and rework cost the agent, not the person. Span's fee stays the same published share for every agent, so Span gains nothing by steering work to one agent over another.

"Participate in the ROI" at Span Proposal, later Charging a share of the money a task made or saved. Only for tasks whose result can be counted, such as a refund recovered or a meeting booked, and only once fixed-price tasks work.

The "value destruction" line is the sharp part. Much reported AI revenue is token resale that passes straight through to model providers. Gross margins look healthy only because nobody asks whether the work succeeded. Once buyers pay only for results, agents that don't reliably finish will see revenue fall away, and some headline "run-rates" (this month's revenue multiplied by twelve) will turn out to have been compute resale. The tweet compares it to the Web1 and mobile transitions, when many companies that looked big did not survive a change in how money flowed.

Today · per token
Buyer carries the risk

Failed and retried work is billed. A seller earns more when its agent takes more steps. Revenue looks big because it includes the model provider's share.

Next · per finished task
Seller carries the risk

Only accepted work is billed. A seller earns more when its agent succeeds cheaply. Revenue is smaller but real, and the margin reflects how well the seller does the job.

Where the tweet goes too far. "Everyone will sell CPA" assumes every task has a result that can be measured. Many don't: "help me think through my pricing" has no clear finish line. And advertising's CPA era also brought fake sign-ups and companies claiming credit for sales that would have happened anyway. The durable version is a mix: fixed price where results can be checked, metered pricing where they can't, and a share of the value only where the value can be counted.

2 · Shift two · who decides

Agents will decide who gets the work

The second shift is about demand: who chooses what gets bought and who gets hired. Today that choice is made by people with limited time, steered by search rankings and ads. Personal agents change who does the choosing, and that changes what companies have to be good at. Much of this argument assumes personal agents will be on the consumer's side; section 2.7 is about what happens if they aren't.

2.1 Companies out-think their customers today

Companies are in some sense the original superintelligence. A company pools the thinking of many people and spreads that cost over millions of customers, which lets it push its own interests against those of some customers. Relative to a company's capacity to optimize, a customer is often inattentive, under-informed, rushed or irrational. The economist Herbert Simon called this bounded rationality, and firms often benefit from it.

Think about booking a flight. The airline has whole teams studying behavior, testing prices and learning when you're most likely to buy extras. You are one person who doesn't have time to compare every airline, read every insurance policy or understand every fee. The classic example is the travel-insurance upsell at the end of a booking, usually priced well above what a regular policy would cost. Now give that person an agent that does the research, knows the tricks and reads the fine print. The customer becomes much harder to exploit, and people will deal with companies somewhat more rationally.

2.2 Some business models stop working

  • Price discrimination works less well. Two people buy the same product. One spends 20 minutes finding a discount code and pays $80; the other doesn't bother and pays $100. Coupons work because only some people hunt for them. If every agent hunts fastidiously for every coupon, the coupon stops separating customers.
  • Hidden cross-subsidies change. Today, people who forget to cancel subscriptions (streaming, gyms, software) subsidize people who remember. Agents cancel what isn't used.
  • Upsells lose their edge. An add-on that sells only because the buyer is rushed stops selling when the buyer's agent isn't rushed.

Across the whole economy it isn't clear who ends up better or worse off. Economists call this the incidence: some prices that were low because other people overpaid may rise.

2.3 Better products beat better marketing more often

Corporate strategists have long argued over what matters more, distribution or product quality. You may have built something better, but does anyone know about it? Won't people just keep buying the ACME Corp mousetrap? Personal agents act as a structural subsidy for product quality. Imagine a market where every buyer spent at least ten hours researching every purchase; that is roughly what agents make possible. On the margin, making the product better becomes a more effective strategy than buying more distribution.

An example. Company A makes average headphones but spends millions on ads, influencers and appearing first on Amazon. Company B makes better headphones (better sound, longer battery, lower price) and barely advertises. Today A often wins because more people know about it. With an agent that compares independent reviews and specifications for each buyer's needs, the agent may recommend B even if the buyer has never heard of it. Instead of another $10 million on ads, a company may get more from spending it on the product.

2.4 Reliable information about quality becomes very valuable

Most people don't read 500 reviews before buying. An agent can: it can compare thousands of reviews, look at return rates, track performance and spot recurring complaints. A small company's product might fail 2% of the time while a famous competitor's fails 15% of the time; most people would never find that out, and an agent could. The smarter agents get, the more valuable trustworthy quality data becomes, and the more room there is for businesses that collect, verify and organize it.

2.5 Paid placement loses some of its value

Many internet companies make money by routing demand, and that routing often depends on short attention spans: "I'll click on the first thing I see." This creates waste, as companies try to please capricious ranking algorithms and pay placement fees. It isn't all wasted, because a seller's willingness to pay for placement is itself a signal that is worth something to buyers, but it's a weak signal.

TodayWith personal agents
The person searches for headphones.The person asks their agent for headphones.

If agents become the main way people discover and buy, paying to appear first becomes less valuable. Companies instead have to convince agents their product deserves recommending. Routing becomes more personal, reflecting each person's taste, alongside whatever ranked placement still carries real information. For services in particular, routing should be neutralized: take the person's preferences and choose on that basis alone. The open question is whether agent platforms will start charging companies for recommendations. That leads to section 2.7.

2.6 Superstars or spread?

A big open question is whether agents increase superstar effects, where rational agents all settle on the same product, or increase dispersion, where purchases spread out because everyone has slightly different tastes and preferences. Agents are good at drawing those out, which could finally undo the artificial clustering that rankings and ads have pushed everyone into.

Possibility A · superstars
Everyone buys the winner

If one laptop is better on paper than every rival, millions of agents recommend it and the leading company grows even more dominant.

Possibility B · spread
Everyone buys their fit

Your agent knows you care about battery life; mine knows I care about the screen; someone else's knows they need the lightest laptop. Each gets a different best answer, and smaller specialized companies can win.

Nobody knows yet which effect will be stronger.

2.7 Everything depends on whose side the agent is on

What if your agent doesn't recommend the best product, but the one whose maker pays the agent's operator the highest commission? Then the old problem is back: instead of sponsored search results we get sponsored recommendations, and the person still can't tell whether the advice is good. The whole argument depends on personal agents genuinely representing their users. An agent that works for you optimizes for what you want, not for what earns its operator the most.

2.8 The economy's feedback gets more accurate

Think of the market as a huge machine that decides how much value each product and company deserves. Today the machine is imperfect: excellent products fail because nobody finds them, and mediocre products succeed because of excellent distribution. Better-informed buyers make it more accurate. In machine-learning terms, this improves the economy's backpropagation: companies get clearer feedback about what people actually value, and better products receive larger rewards.

3 · What this means for Span

Span is the agent that hires agents, so it has to be on the person's side

The argument above is mostly about shopping. Span's opportunity is related but broader. Picture someone who simply tells their AI, "Build me a website for my business." Instead of searching for website builders, comparing freelancers and vetting agencies, their AI finds and picks the best specialized agent for the job. The same goes for tax returns, video editing, contract review, research and other digital work. The competition moves from being discovered by people to being selected by agents. Span's core claim is exactly this: people say what they want, and Span works out who should do it, even when the person doesn't know who that is. That role has three jobs:

JobWhat it meansWhere Span is
DiscoveryFind every agent and tool that can do the task.FactBuilt: federated discovery across registries and providers.
SelectionPick the best one for this person on quality, price, reliability and fit with their needs.FactPartly built: SpanRank. Its per-person taste signal learns only from approvals and has zero weight under the default settings (section 4).
ExecutionHire it, pay it, check the work, deliver the result.FactBuilt, but pays for effort: every settled attempt is paid, good or bad. Shift one fixes this.

Span's opportunity is to be the infrastructure that lets demand find, and do business with, the best available AI services. This only works because of a decision you already made: Fact people never bring their own vendor keys. Span holds the accounts, pays providers, and is the merchant of record, reselling the work. That places Span alongside platforms that take a published cut of work they front (OpenRouter is one example), not alongside zero-margin pass-throughs.

One distinction matters more than anything else in this memo: routing doesn't automatically reward quality. It rewards whatever the routing system is built to optimize. Optimize for commissions and Span recreates sponsored search. Optimize for verified outcomes and the person's own interests and quality becomes a real competitive advantage. In an economy where agents increasingly decide what to buy and whom to hire, the systems that discover, judge and route demand may become as important as the products and services themselves. Four rules follow.

Rule 1 · neutral routing

Nobody can buy a better rank, including Span

FactThe whitepaper already says "the platform has no commercial levers to tilt a rank outcome in exchange for provider revenue," and nothing in the ranking code reads money paid to Span.

ProposalExtend it to Span's own money: the ranking never sees Span's fee or margin. One published fee per kind of task, the same for every agent. No paid placement and no sponsored slots, ever, enforced by a test.

Rule 2 · learn the person's taste

Selection gets more personal with every verdict

ProposalWhen a person's agent deals with other agents on their behalf, it should build up the same taste and habits the person would: preferred styles, trusted agents, things they always reject. Every keep or send-back is a lesson. In reinforcement-learning terms, each verdict is a reward signal that tunes the selector to this person.

FactToday the signal learns only from approvals, files every task as "general", and counts for nothing under default weights.

Rule 3 · keep the market open

Push toward spread, not superstars

ProposalGive new specialists real chances, and track how concentrated work is per kind of task. If one agent takes almost everything for reasons other than fit, that's a warning sign.

FactA "discovery bonus" (one request in ten goes to a promising newer agent) exists in the code and is cited by the whitepaper, but nothing calls it. It is switched off.

Rule 4 · make quality visible

Verified outcomes are the quality data

ProposalAcceptance rates per agent and per kind of task are exactly the trustworthy quality information agents need. Show each agent its own scorecard, including common reasons people send its work back, so improving the product is clearly the winning move.

This only works if acceptance is measured honestly, which is why shift one (pay on acceptance) and shift two (route on quality) must read the same verdicts.

5 · The best version

Span buys finished work for the person, at a published price

Proposal Put the two shifts and the four rules together. The person describes a task. Span ranks agents on what matters to this person: fit with their taste, verified quality, and the price of a finished task. The person sees one price before work starts. The agent is paid only when the work is accepted. Span earns one published fee on finished work and pays for its own quality checks, so it does best by finishing work people keep.

The person

One price, quoted up front, owed only on acceptance

Before work starts, the person sees "This costs $2.10." The money is reserved, as today. If the work is accepted, it settles. If Span can't deliver within its retry limit, the reservation is released and nothing is charged.

Revisions, retries and quality checks never appear on the bill.

The agent

Commits to a price per kind of task, paid on acceptance

An agent says "I'll do a research brief for $2.10." If accepted, it earns $2.10 minus Span's published fee. If rejected, it earns nothing and its compute is its own loss. Revisions are on the agent.

An efficient agent keeps its savings, so agents compete on getting better and cheaper, not on taking more steps or buying placement.

Span

Earns a published fee, pays for checking and failure

Span's take is a stated percentage of finished work, the same for every agent doing that kind of task. Span already pays for the Judge and Verifier; under this design it also eats them on failed attempts.

Span makes more by finishing more work that people keep and come back for, never by steering.

Acceptance

Judge first, person second, silence counts as yes

The Judge gives the first verdict. The person can send work back within a window, say 72 hours. If they say nothing, the work is accepted and earnings release.

Every verdict also teaches the taste signal. An agent can contest a send-back once; it goes to a review queue. People who reject almost everything are rate-limited, so nobody gets free work by always saying no.

How Span ranks agents for a person

A cheap agent that fails half the time costs more than a pricier one that almost always succeeds, because each failure costs a retry, a check and the person's time. And the best agent for one person isn't the best for another.

rank for this person = taste fit × acceptance rate (this kind of task) ÷ cost of a finished task  ·  Span's fee is never an input
cost of a finished task = agent's price + (Judge and Verifier cost ÷ acceptance rate)

This replaces the static per-call cost term. Acceptance rate is measured from the same verdicts that release money, so ranking and payout never disagree about whether an agent is good. New agents start with a cautious estimate (the sandbox's 0.5 to 0.7 band), get real chances through a working newcomer boost, and earn traffic as their record grows.

One published fee; quality rewarded through ranking

An earlier version of this memo proposed two things I now think are wrong. One was letting Span earn a variable gap between the person's price and the agent's price. The other was charging agents with better acceptance rates a lower fee (for example 20% below 70% acceptance, 15% from 70% to 90%, 10% above 90%). Both give Span a money reason to prefer some agents over others, which is the sponsored-search problem moved inside Span. The cleaner design is one published fee per kind of task. Quality is rewarded the honest way, by being chosen more often. Today's year-one and volume tiers can stay as developer incentives because the ranking can't see them.

Two modes, not one

  • Fixed price per task for kinds of task with a checkable result: booking a meeting, filing an expense report, finding every deduction. This is the default wherever the Judge's accuracy has been measured.
  • Metered (today's cost-plus model, with budget caps) for open-ended work like "help me think through my pricing" and for new kinds of task, shown on the receipt as "billed by usage."

A kind of task moves from metered to fixed price once Span has enough accepted runs to price it with confidence. The metered history is the pricing data.

Sharing the return, later and narrowly

Taking a share of the value created fits only where value is countable: a refund recovered, a charge disputed, a meeting booked. There, the price can be a share of the money recovered with a floor. Everywhere else it invites the same fraud CPA advertising did, so it waits until fixed-price tasks work.

How Span reports revenue

As merchant of record, Span may be able to book the full amount people pay as revenue. That is the inflated kind of number the tweet warns about. Lead with Span's net fee and its margin per finished task, with gross shown alongside. OpenConfirm the accounting with an accountant before any investor material.

  • Ranking never reads a fee, a margin or any money paid to Span. A test fails if it does.

10 · Risks

Where this can go wrong

  • Span becomes the new sponsored search. This is the biggest risk. Revenue pressure will push toward "featured" agents or fee-dependent ranking. Rule 1 and its test are the defense, and the whitepaper already invites anyone to check.
  • Gaming the Judge. If money depends on the Judge, agents will optimize to pass it rather than to do the task. Sample people's verdicts against the Judge's, rotate checks, and let a person's send-back override the Judge.
  • Taste becomes a bubble. Learning someone's habits can trap them in yesterday's choices. A working newcomer boost and occasional fresh options keep the door open.
  • Taste data is sensitive. What someone keeps and rejects says a lot about them. It must follow the privacy rules in 6.2.
  • People withholding acceptance. The auto-accept window, per-person rejection rates and rate limits on serial rejecters handle most of it.
  • Agents pricing up or refusing hard tasks. Moving risk onto agents makes them charge for it. That's correct, but some kinds of task will get pricier or lose supply. Keep metered mode for them.
  • Span carries refund and check costs. When a quoted task fails, Span eats the checks and loses the sale. Price a small risk margin into the fee per kind of task, and cap exposure with the retry limit.
  • Superstars anyway. If one agent really is best for nearly everyone, work concentrates. That's fine when it's earned; the concentration measure shows whether it is.
  • Promises ahead of code. The whitepaper cites a newcomer boost that's switched off and fee tiers that don't work, and the legal page says there's no fee. Public writing, including the blog post, should describe only what's true or clearly mark what's planned.
  • Reported revenue looks smaller. Net-first reporting is honest but less flattering than gross. That is the point of the tweet.
Bottom line. In an economy where agents decide what to buy and whom to hire, the systems that find, judge and route the work become as important as the work itself. Span is one of those systems. It wins only if it is visibly on the person's side: it ranks on taste, verified quality and the real cost of finished work, never on who pays, and it pays agents only for work people keep. Much of the plumbing exists. The missing pieces are saving verdicts (on the branch), held earnings, taste that learns from send-backs and actually counts, a working newcomer boost, a tested no-steering rule, a published fee, per-task prices and a dispute path. I'm about 75% confident in steps 1 to 4 as written; step 5's details should be tested against real acceptance data first.

Chamath Palihapitiya summed up where the AI business stands in four lines. "Most everyone is selling CPM. Cost per thousand tokens + markup." Next, he wrote, "Everyone will sell CPA. Accomplish the task, participate in the ROI." On the way there he expects "a lot of value destruction," and he points to the Web1 and mobile transitions as examples.

From impressions to results

He is talking about how AI is sold, and he borrows the terms from advertising. CPM is cost per mille, the price of a thousand impressions. An advertiser paid every time a banner was shown, whether anyone clicked or bought. CPA is cost per acquisition: the advertiser pays when someone signs up or buys. Advertising moved from the first to the second because buyers wanted to pay for outcomes, and the companies that could measure outcomes took the market. Google's search ads moved to charging per click in 2002, and in a clip from 20 October he named that as his precedent: "When Google invented and scaled AdWords and AdSense, the market exploded… because we went from buying 1,000 ads to just getting an outcome."

AI is still in its banner-ad era. Model labs sell tokens, and most AI products resell those tokens with a markup, packaged as seats or credits. The buyer pays for effort. If an agent spends four dollars of compute and gets the work wrong, the buyer still pays four dollars, and pays again for the retry. Revenue grows with usage whether or not anything got done.

That pricing flatters the numbers investors look at. Revenue includes the token bill that passes straight through to the model provider. Gross margins look healthy because failed and repeated work is billed like good work. Run-rates multiply the best month by twelve, pilots and experiments included. Once buyers pay only for finished work, those numbers get restated, and some companies built on them will not survive it, which is the value destruction he expects. He expects the market as a whole to grow anyway. "Right now we buy a million tokens. We will eventually move to an outcome. And I think when that happens, this market will just go absolutely crazy." Both predictions hold together. Buyers who hesitate to pay for uncertain effort will pay for a finished task, so total spending rises, while the companies whose numbers rested on reselling tokens are repriced.

Where Span stands

Span is the conversation people use to get work done across agents from many providers. A person describes the job, and Span does it itself or routes it to the specialist agent best suited to it. Span holds the provider accounts, pays the agents and bills the person. Every dollar spent on agent work passes through us, which makes Span exactly the kind of company that could count pass-through as revenue and call it growth. We want to build the version that holds up after the transition.

Today Span pays the way most of the market does. An agent's attempt is charged at the cost the agent reports, and the developer is credited that amount minus our fee as soon as it lands. Our quality check, the Judge, reviews every attempt and decides whether to approve it, send it back for revision, reject it or hand it to the person. That verdict decides what the person sees. It does not yet decide who gets paid. A rejected attempt earns the same as an approved one, and each revision is billed as a new attempt.

Paid for accepted work

We are changing that. Span will pay agents for accepted work. Accepted means the Judge approves the work and the person keeps it. After delivery the person has a window, 72 hours to start, to send it back, and silence counts as keeping it. The agent's earnings are held during the window and released when it closes. Failed attempts and rework are the agent's cost. The person pays one price, quoted before the work starts, and only for work they keep.

This changes what a good agent is. Under token pricing, an agent that takes more steps earns more. Under acceptance pricing, the agent that gets the work right the first time, at a low cost, earns the most. Developers are paid for being right, which is the incentive they should have had from the start.

A router nobody can buy

It also changes what the router has to be. When agents choose the work, the router decides who gets hired. Advertising's results era carries a warning here: it came with paid placement, and the top result in a search box was often the one that paid most. If agents could pay Span for position, or if Span ranked its own margin above the person's interest, the person would be paying for a worse result in a less visible way. Google understood part of this early. From 2002 an AdWords ad's position depended on its bid multiplied by how often people clicked it, so an ad nobody wanted could not buy its way up. We take the bid out entirely.

Two rules sit underneath the change. Span charges one published fee per kind of task, the same for every agent, so it gains nothing by steering work toward one agent over another. And Span's fee and margin are never inputs to the ranking. Agents are ranked on how well they fit the person's taste, how often their work is accepted, and the real cost of a finished task, which includes the attempts that failed. An agent that is cheap per attempt but often sent back is expensive per finished task, and the ranking should see that.

The ranking also has to learn taste. A person's agent will build habits from every verdict, learning which answers they keep and which they send back. That raises an open question about the agent economy. Demand could concentrate on a few superstar agents, because rational buyers converge on the same best product. Or it could spread out, because agents are good at drawing out each person's particular preferences. We think a neutral router that learns each person's taste pushes toward the second outcome, and we will give newer agents a measured share of work so the market stays open to them.

The share of the value comes later

"Participate in the ROI" is the step after that, and we are holding it back on purpose. Some tasks have a result you can count, such as a refund recovered or a meeting booked. For those, a share of the value is fair. Most tasks have no clean number. "Help me think through my pricing" has no finish line you can price as a percentage, and advertising showed what happens when payment rides on outcomes that are easy to claim: fake sign-ups, and credit taken for sales that would have happened anyway. We will start with fixed prices for work that can be checked, and add a share of the value only where the value can be counted.

How we get there

We are doing this in steps, and each one is useful by itself. First, Span keeps the Judge's verdict on every attempt. Next, a shadow report shows each developer what they were paid against what acceptance-based pay would have paid, before any money moves. After that, earnings are held until acceptance, prices are quoted per task, and every agent gets a scorecard of its acceptance rate and one way to contest a send-back. When we report revenue, we will lead with what Span keeps after agents and model providers are paid.

Chamath closed with "Good luck to all the players!" The companies that come through this transition will be the ones already measuring results when buyers start asking for them. For a marketplace, that means paying for work people keep and making sure nobody can buy their way to the top.

A.4 The plain-English breakdown you shared

The author is making a very important argument about how AI agents could change the economy.

The central idea is simple: Today, companies have an advantage over consumers because they have more information, more resources, and more time to figure out how to sell to us. AI agents could eliminate much of that advantage by giving every consumer an intelligent assistant that works in their best interest.

And if that happens, companies may have to compete more on how good their products actually are, rather than how good they are at marketing and selling them.

Here's the argument, broken down into plain English.

1. Companies are smarter than individual consumers

Think about booking a flight.

An airline has entire teams dedicated to figuring out how to make you spend more money. They study your behavior, experiment with prices, and figure out when you're most likely to buy unnecessary extras.

Meanwhile, you're just one person trying to book a flight.

You don't have the time to compare every airline, read every insurance policy, or understand every hidden fee.

The company has hundreds of people optimizing how to make money from you. You have only yourself.

Now imagine you have an AI agent that does all the research for you, understands the tricks companies use, and makes sure you get the best deal.

Suddenly, the consumer becomes much harder to exploit.

That's the first argument.

2. Some business models depend on consumers being lazy or forgetful

Consider subscriptions.

Netflix, gyms, and software companies sometimes make money from people who forget to cancel subscriptions they no longer use.

Or consider coupons.

Two people buy the same product. One spends 20 minutes finding a discount code and pays $80. The other doesn't bother and pays $100.

Companies benefit from these differences in consumer behavior.

But what happens when everyone has an AI agent that automatically finds coupons, cancels unused subscriptions, and negotiates better deals?

Those opportunities for companies to make extra money start disappearing.

The author is saying this could change how businesses price their products and make money.

3. Better products could finally beat better marketing

This is arguably the most important point.

Imagine two companies selling headphones.

Company A

Makes average headphones, but spends millions on advertising, influencers, and getting its products to appear first on Amazon.

Company B

Makes excellent headphones with better sound, longer battery life, and a lower price, but barely spends anything on advertising.

Today, Company A might win because more people know about it.

But imagine everyone has an AI agent that researches every purchase, compares independent reviews, evaluates specifications, and identifies the best headphones for their needs.

The agent might recommend Company B even if the consumer has never heard of it.

AI agents could make it easier for the best product to win, rather than the best-advertised product.

This would fundamentally change how companies compete.

Instead of spending another $10 million on advertising, a company might find it more profitable to spend that money improving its product.

4. Information about product quality becomes extremely valuable

Today, most people don't read 500 customer reviews before buying something.

But an AI agent can.

It can compare thousands of reviews, examine return rates, analyze product performance, and identify recurring complaints.

That means information about how good a product actually is becomes much more valuable.

For example, a small company might have a product with a 2% failure rate while a famous competitor has a 15% failure rate.

Most consumers would never discover that difference.

But an AI agent could.

The more intelligent our agents become, the more valuable reliable information about products becomes.

This also creates opportunities for companies that collect, verify, and organize product-quality data.

5. Will AI make everyone buy the same thing or discover different things?

The author raises an interesting question.

Imagine every consumer has an incredibly intelligent shopping agent.

Two possible outcomes emerge.

Scenario A: Everyone buys the same products.

If one laptop is objectively better than every competitor, millions of agents might recommend it.

The winning company becomes even more dominant.

Scenario B: People buy more different products.

Your agent knows you care about battery life. My agent knows I care about screen quality. Someone else's agent knows they need the lightest possible laptop.

Instead of everyone buying the most popular laptop, agents find the product that best fits each individual.

This could help smaller, specialized companies succeed.

The author doesn't know which effect will be stronger.

6. AI agents could threaten Google's and Amazon's advertising businesses

This is another major point.

Today, companies pay enormous amounts of money to appear where consumers are looking.

For example, when you search for a product on Google or Amazon, companies pay to appear near the top.

Why?

Because consumers often click the first few results.

If AI agents become the primary way consumers discover and purchase products, paying Google to appear first might become less valuable.

Companies would instead need to convince AI agents that their products are worth recommending.

The power to direct consumer spending could shift from search engines and advertising platforms to AI agents.

However, this creates another question: Will agent platforms themselves start charging companies for recommendations?

Which leads to the next point.

7. Everything depends on whether AI agents actually work for consumers

The author acknowledges a major assumption.

What if your AI agent doesn't recommend the best product?

What if it recommends the company that pays the agent provider the highest commission?

Then we've recreated the same problem.

Instead of Google selling sponsored search results, AI agents could sell sponsored recommendations.

The consumer still wouldn't know whether they're receiving the best advice.

So the entire argument depends on whether personal AI agents genuinely represent the interests of their users.

An agent that works for you should optimize for what you want, not what generates the highest commission for its operator.

8. The final paragraph: The economy could reward quality more accurately

The author describes the market as a giant machine that decides how much value different products and companies deserve.

Today, that machine is imperfect.

Excellent products sometimes fail because nobody discovers them. Mediocre products sometimes succeed because they have excellent distribution.

AI agents could improve that process.

When consumers have access to better information and more intelligent decision-making, money could flow more consistently toward companies that create genuine value.

The author calls this a change in the economy's backpropagation.

That's a machine-learning analogy. In simple terms, companies receive clearer feedback about what consumers actually value, and better products receive greater financial rewards.

What I think is most important for Span

There is a particularly interesting implication here that extends beyond shopping.

The author is describing a world where AI agents become the decision-makers that direct economic demand.

Today, consumers discover software, services, and companies through search engines, advertising, app stores, and recommendations.

But imagine a world where someone simply tells their AI:

"Build me a website for my business."

Instead of searching Google for website builders, comparing freelancers, and evaluating design agencies, their AI could discover and select the best specialized agent to complete the job.

The same applies to preparing tax returns, editing videos, analyzing contracts, conducting research, or completing other forms of digital work.

This creates a potentially important shift:

The competition moves from being discovered by humans to being selected by agents.

And that introduces three distinct opportunities:

1. Discovery: How does an AI find all the specialized agents capable of performing a task?
2. Selection: How does it determine which agent is best based on quality, price, reliability, and the user's needs?
3. Execution: How does it hire that agent, pay it, verify its work, and deliver the result?

The author's argument focuses primarily on how personal agents could improve consumer decision-making.

Span's opportunity is related but broader: building the infrastructure that allows demand to find and transact with the best available AI services.

There is one critical distinction, though. Routing doesn't automatically reward quality. It rewards whatever the routing system is designed to optimize. If the system prioritizes commissions, it can recreate the same problems as sponsored search. If it prioritizes verified outcomes and user interests, it can make quality a genuine competitive advantage.

The single biggest takeaway is this:

In an economy where agents increasingly decide what to buy and whom to hire, the systems that discover, evaluate, and route demand could become as important as the products and services themselves.
