Prices that move: dynamic pricing and hidden markups

Chamath's point is about the unit of sale: a thousand ads then, a million tokens now, an outcome next. Under it sits a second question. Who sets the price, and does it change depending on who is buying? Three different practices get called "dynamic pricing". Two of them are ordinary business. The third is the one people mean when they say the airline raised the fare because of their cookies.

The airline cookie story

For airlines, the cookie story is mostly a myth, and the evidence is consistent. Consumer Reports ran 372 matched searches in 2016, one browser with cookies and one wiped. The fare was identical 88% of the time. The wiped browser was cheaper 7% of the time and dearer 5% of the time, which is noise. Fares move because seats sell and the flight gets closer, and searching again a minute later often hits a fare class that just sold out. That is demand pricing, the first row.

The worry is closer to coming true now. Delta prices part of its fares with an AI tool from the Israeli company Fetcherr: about 3% of domestic fares in mid-2025, with a goal of 20% by the end of that year. Its president described fares set "for that flight, on that time, to you, the individual." After pushback from Congress, Delta wrote on 18 September 2026 that the tool gets only route-level demand and market data and no personal information. The rules are tightening. New York's Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act has been enforced since 10 November 2025: a price set from personal data must carry the label "THIS PRICE WAS SET BY AN ALGORITHM USING YOUR PERSONAL DATA", with fines up to $1,000 per violation. On 19 August 2026 the FTC proposed a policy saying that varying prices on personal data without clear disclosure may break federal law.

Where AI pricing sits today

Model providers publish one token price for everyone. The movement is in discounts for time and patience: batch jobs at half price at Anthropic and OpenAI, repeated (cached) input billed at a fraction of the price, and off-peak discounts at DeepSeek. All of that is demand pricing, and it is public. The markup is hidden one layer up, in apps and agents that resell tokens. The buyer sees a credit pack or a monthly fee and cannot tell what share went to the model.

Paying by outcome changes this. Once a buyer pays per finished task, the price is no longer tokens times a public list price. Span and the developer set it, and the buyer has no meter to check it against. That makes the other two rows easier to slip into: a markup nobody can see, and a price that depends on who is asking. Ad networks show where this leads. Buyers trusted cost per action because the price was set per action in an open auction. A price per person would have looked like a trick.

Proposed rule (your call): a task's price may depend on the task, how urgent it is and how busy the system is. It is quoted before work starts, as the developer's price plus Span's stated fee, and anyone asking for the same task at the same moment gets the same quote. It never depends on the person: their history, device, location, past spending or a guess at what they will pay. This keeps Span outside New York's label law and the FTC's proposal, and it matches the merchant-of-record decision of 24 September 2026, under which Span bills with a stated fee.
